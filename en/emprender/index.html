Five women, with their sources

They built a business without permission

Do you feel like you need permission to sell what you know? So many of us do. A hundred and a hundred and fifty years ago, the law or custom shut the door on these women. They made a living from their work anyway. Here's how.

  1. Brazil · over a hundred years ago

    Chiquinha Gonzaga

    What she did
    Composer and pianist. She made a living teaching piano and playing at dances. She sold her sheet music and was the first woman to conduct an orchestra in a theater in Brazil.
    The wall
    Her father married her off at 16. When she left her husband, her father disinherited her. She was left without her family's help.
    How she got over it
    She started with what she already knew how to do: teach. She placed an ad in the newspaper offering lessons. Years later, she helped found the society that protects the rights of theater authors.
    For today
    Your first service can be what you already know how to teach.
  2. Spain · over a hundred and fifty years ago

    Fermina Orduña

    What she did
    She lived in Madrid. She invented a special carriage to take donkeys, cows or goats around the city and sell their milk to the public.
    The wall
    Inventing and registering inventions was men's business. Many women inventors of the time stayed hidden behind the name of a husband, a father or a relative.
    How she got over it
    She asked for protection of the invention in her own name, and she got it: she is the first Spanish woman to register an invention of her own at the patent office.
    For today
    A small problem on your street can become a business. And what you create carries your name.
  3. Peru · over a hundred years ago

    Clorinda Matto de Turner

    What she did
    Writer and journalist. She ran newspapers in Cusco, Arequipa and Lima. She was the only woman at the head of a prestigious weekly in Lima.
    The wall
    She published a story that caused a scandal, and she had to step down as the paper's editor. Years later, because of politics, she had to leave the country.
    How she got over it
    She set up her own printing press and printed her own newspaper there. Later, in Buenos Aires, she founded another magazine that lasted twelve years.
    For today
    If they close a door on you, you can build your own workshop. Even a small one.
  4. Colombia · almost a hundred and fifty years ago

    Soledad Acosta de Samper

    What she did
    Writer, journalist, editor and publishing entrepreneur. She founded and ran five magazines. The first was called La Mujer (The Woman).
    The wall
    The law in her country put a married woman under her husband's permission to manage her property and sign documents. That didn't change until less than a hundred years ago.
    How she got over it
    She didn't wait for the law to change. She wrote for women and families, and she built her own magazines, one after another, for almost thirty years.
    For today
    You don't need an invitation. You can create your own channel for the people you want to help.
  5. Chile · over a hundred and fifty years ago

    Isidora Goyenechea

    What she did
    She ran the coal mining company in Lota. She commissioned Chile's first hydroelectric plant and built schools and a home for miners.
    The wall
    Under the Civil Code, a married woman couldn't run her own business: the law put her alongside minors. And she had no experience running companies.
    How she got over it
    When she was widowed, the law no longer put her under anyone. She took over the company and learned by running it.
    For today
    You don't have to know everything before you start. You learn by doing.

Your first-step sheet for this week

Five questions, five minutes

Answer them on paper or in your notes. You don't need to have a business. Just take one step.

  1. What do you know how to do that someone has already asked you for?Like Chiquinha with the piano.
  2. What small problem do you see every week on your street, at work or in your group?Like Fermina with the milk.
  3. What door feels closed to you, and what could you build yourself, even if it's small?Like Clorinda with her printing press.
  4. Which three people could you tell about what you offer this week?Write down their names.
  5. What thirty-minute step will you take before Sunday, and on what day will you do it?A message, a post, a conversation.

This sheet doesn't promise income. It's for getting started, the way they did.
