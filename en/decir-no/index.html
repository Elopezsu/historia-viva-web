No apologies

Seven phrases for saying no at work

For the 9 p.m. message, the meeting nobody needs, the free work and the “just a tiny favor.” Copy them as they are. You can use them tomorrow.

Before you start

A clear no is short. It doesn't come with “sorry,” it doesn't come with three explanations and it doesn't need the other person to agree. If you want, offer another way out. If not, that's fine too.

  1. The after-hours request Got it. I'll look at it first thing tomorrow and send it to you before ten.

    You don't say “I can't right now, sorry.” You say when you can. That's setting the limit and coming across well at the same time.

  2. The meeting that could have been an email What do you need to decide with me? If it's a piece of information, I'll send it in writing and we can skip the meeting.

    You're not rejecting the person: you're offering a faster way to get what they're after.

  3. The free work This is something I offer as a service. I'll send you a proposal and we'll take it from there.

    It works for the “can you help me with a little thing in your field?” Your work has a name and it has a price.

  4. The “just a tiny favor” I can't this week. If you'd like, I can tell you who could help.

    A no with an open door. And if you don't know anyone, the first sentence is enough on its own.

  5. One more task on top of everything I can, but tell me what to push back: this, or what you asked me for on Monday?

    Your time doesn't stretch. Let the other person choose what matters more.

  6. The client who asks for “one more change” That wasn't part of what we agreed. I'll quote it separately.

    It's not picking a fight. It's going back to what you both already signed.

  7. The “I need your answer now” Let me look at it calmly and I'll get back to you tomorrow.

    Someone else's rush doesn't have to be your emergency. A rushed yes costs more than a timely no.

They said no before we did

The three women from the reel. And what each one teaches your next no.

  1. ECUADOR · A HUNDRED YEARS AGO

    Matilde Hidalgo

    A physician. She went to register to vote and was told that women didn't vote. She asked where that was written. The Constitution required being 21 years old and knowing how to read and write. It didn't say you had to be a man. She insisted, and she voted.

    For your no: ask about the rule. “Where does it say this is my job?”

  2. MEXICO · MORE THAN THREE HUNDRED YEARS AGO

    Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz

    A bishop, signing with a nun's name, wrote to tell her to give up her studies of worldly things. She answered with a long, calm letter defending her right, and every woman's right, to learn.

    For your no: put it in writing, calmly and with your reasons. Without apologizing for thinking.

  3. PERU · ALMOST 250 YEARS AGO

    Micaela Bastidas

    In the rebellion against Spanish rule, she handled what no one sees: she got the food, the money, the clothing and the weapons, and she sent the messages. She paid for it with her life.

    For your no: a no holds up when you're clear about what you're protecting.

Saying no doesn't make you selfish.
