The two laws, with their sources

Until 1989, your wallet wasn't yours

Chile and Colombia used the same Civil Code, the one written by Andrés Bello. Both treated married women the same way. Colombia changed it in 1932. Chile took 57 more years.

  1. 1855 · 1873

    One code for both countries

    Chile passed Andrés Bello's Civil Code in 1855, and Colombia adopted it in 1873. In both, a married woman was on the list of the "relatively incapable," next to minors and people a court had barred from handling their own money. Her legal representative was her husband. To sell, buy, sign or go to court, she needed his permission.

  2. COLOMBIA · 1932

    Law 28 of 1932

    Issued on November 12, 1932. From then on, each spouse managed their own property, and a married woman no longer needed permission.

    "A married woman of legal age, as such, may freely appear in court, and to manage and dispose of her property she needs neither her husband's authorization nor a judge's license, nor will her husband be her legal representative."Law 28 of 1932, article 5 (my translation)
  3. CHILE · 1989

    Law 18.802 of 1989

    Published in the Diario Oficial (the official gazette) on June 9, 1989, in force from September 8. It took married women off the list of the "relatively incapable" in article 1447 of the Civil Code, and it took husbands off the list of their legal representatives, in article 43.

    Article 1447, before and afterBefore: the relatively incapable are adult minors, spendthrifts under court interdiction, and married women. After: only adult minors and spendthrifts.
What the law didn't change overnight

In Chile, since 1925, a married woman who worked could manage what she earned from her work. It was called the patrimonio reservado (her "reserved property"). And after 1989, in the marital partnership, the husband was still the one who managed the shared property. The incapacity ended in 1989. Equality took longer.

This isn't an opinion.

It's the Civil Code.
