Where does the «de» come from?

In Spanish-speaking countries, a married woman added «de» (“of”) and her husband’s surname after her own. In Colombia, a married woman’s surname wasn’t always the same. This is what researchers Carmen Diana Deere and Magdalena León found in archives, laws, and interviews.

  1. COLONIAL ERA

    Married women kept their surname

    Between 1812 and 1820, in the petitions women made to the State, more than half of married women and widows appear under their maiden name, and almost none used the «de». Nor was that «de» a sign of being married: it showed up in compound surnames, for men and women alike.

  2. NINETEENTH CENTURY

    The «de» arrives with the Republic

    It starts after Independence, among elite women. Of twelve well-known Colombian women writers of the century, ten used it. By the end of the century it was the norm among middle- and upper-class married women, along with the idea of the wife as “queen of the home.”

  3. 1934

    It becomes mandatory by law

    Decree 540 of 1934 ordered married women and widows to use “her name and her husband’s surname, preceded by the particle de.” It passed without anyone commenting on it, just two years after Law 28 of 1932 gave married women the right to manage their own property. Decree 1003 of 1939 repeated the requirement.

  4. 1970

    It stops being mandatory

    Decree 1260 of 1970 says that “every person has the right to their individuality.” From then on, the «de» becomes an option. In practice, dropping it was still a tangled bureaucratic process.

  5. 1987 · 1988

    Add it or drop it, by deed

    In 1987 the Registraduría (Colombia’s civil registry office) made the change on the national ID card simpler. Decree 999 of 1988 set the final path: a married woman can add or drop the «de» with a public deed before a notary.

  6. TODAY

    It’s your choice, and there’s more than one way to live it

    “Not one owning the other, but a chain that binds us together.”Lucy, who uses the «de» because she likes it
    “Here I am me, period. I don’t belong to anyone anymore.”Marta, who went back to her own surname

From 1934 to 1970, the law didn’t let them choose.

They couldn’t choose. Today, you can.

Source: Carmen Diana Deere y Magdalena León (2023), «Identidad de las mujeres casadas: el uso del “de” en sus apellidos en Colombia», Revista de Estudios Sociales 84: 19-39. doi.org/10.7440/res84.2023.02. The interviewees’ names are the ones used in the article.
